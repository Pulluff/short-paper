## Summary

Halpern and Pearl ask whether a particular event caused an outcome in a specified situation. Structural equations describe how variables respond to one another. Holding the background context fixed, an intervention replaces the equation for a selected variable with an assigned value. Other variables can then respond to that change. A cause may become decisive only under a contingency, for example when another sufficient cause is absent. [§2, pp. 847–849](actcaus.pdf#page=5) · [§3, Definition 3.1, p. 853](actcaus.pdf#page=10)

## Passages to cite

- **The actual-cause test, draft §2.** AC1 requires both the proposed cause and outcome to have occurred. AC2(a) asks whether changing the candidate under a contingency makes the outcome false. AC2(b) restores the candidate to its actual value and checks that the outcome persists across subsets of the contingency and restorations of actual process variables. AC3 excludes redundant parts of the candidate cause. [Definition 3.1, p. 853](actcaus.pdf#page=10)
- **Why removing one cause can miss its role, draft §2.** In the forest-fire example, either of two lit matches can suffice. With both lit, removing one leaves the fire. Holding the second match unlit makes the first decisive. The authors also analyze a version in which both matches are required, so removing either prevents the fire. [Example 3.2 and Fig. 2, pp. 856–857](actcaus.pdf#page=13)
- **Which contingencies count, draft §5.** The authors label Definition 3.1 preliminary. Section 5 refines it by allowing the model to restrict which settings may be used in the counterfactual test. [§5, p. 870](actcaus.pdf#page=25)

**Use in the motor argument.** A temperature that remains above 90 °C after demagnetization is removed answers the direct necessity question for that run. A claim about demagnetization under the full Halpern–Pearl definition would also require specifying the model and examining its allowable contingencies. [Motor study, Eq.
